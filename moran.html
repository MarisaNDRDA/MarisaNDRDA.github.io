The full story

I’m building a MATLAB pipeline that models how retinal ganglion cells respond to visual stimuli, using multielectrode array recordings from rat and tree shrew retinas. The pipeline converts XML stimulus movies into a movie buffer, computes spike-triggered averages to map each cell’s receptive field, and fits a Linear-Nonlinear-Poisson (LNP) model with spatial and temporal filters and a nonlinear generator signal. I then added a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) stage that refines predictions so the model can forecast a cell’s spike train from the stimulus alone across repeated movies. Extending the model from rat to tree shrew supports the lab’s glaucoma research.

Background

The retina is a sophisticated neural circuit that performs a variety of computations on input stimuli before visual signals reach the brain. Although major retinal pathways are well studied, the small-scale variations and irregularities in how these cells interact, known as retinal circuit anomalies, remain poorly understood. These anomalies directly influence how visual information is processed within and across cell populations (Demb & Singer, 2015). Understanding these computations is key to identifying how the retina encodes natural signals in healthy state and how encoding is disrupted in diseased state, such as in glaucoma.

Most existing retinal computational models describe retinal functions in a rod-dominated system, such as the rat retina, which has evolved for supporting low-light vision. These models include Linear-Nonlinear-Poisson cascade (LNP) and Generalized Linear Models (GLM), which provide a strong basis for canonical retinal computations based on stimulus-response relationships (Chichilnisky, 2001). However, they do not capture the temporal precision, nonlinear response properties, and circuit level dynamics of cone-dominated retinas suited for diurnal vision, the regime that closely reflects conditions encountered by the human visual system. This leaves a significant gap in our understanding of how retinal circuits process natural stimuli in diurnal conditions, specifically in the fovea, where high-acuity visual processing occurs.

Existing LNP and GLM implementations described in literature and that are publicly available in codebases have been utilized to model neural spike trains, but they are not designed to fit the data the Roy lab produces. Closing this gap required developing new LNP and GLM models from the ground up, specifically adapted to the lab’s specific structure: white noise, white noise repeats, and natural movies. In the resulting scripts, the LNP model uses the white noise stimulus to compute a spike-triggered average (STA), the cell’s estimated receptive field, which is validated against the STA produced by the Vision software; this becomes the linear filter. A fitted nonlinearity then converts the filtered stimulus into a predicted firing rate. The parallel GLM uses the same stimulus and receptive field within its regression framework. Building both models to operate reliably on the lab’s multielectrode array (MEA) data recordings was the substantial foundational work of the previous award.
