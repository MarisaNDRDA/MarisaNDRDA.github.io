A polarized microscope I built

To confirm that the algae I collected was Cladophora glomerata, I improvised a polarized-light microscope from 2 SPIE polarizers, 2 half-wave plates, and LEDs with paper as a diffuser.

Healthy C. glomerata showed its birefringent property under polarized light, and a wave plate changed the color response of bleached samples.

Why it mattersApplying optics (polarization and wave plates) to a biology question, using materials on hand.

What changed

  • Day 1 (Jul 21, 2026): standard setup with a tripod and Pika L stage, the imager as close to nadir as possible over the tank. Clear, sunny sky. Images were unusable due to sky glint.
  • Day 3 (Jul 24, 2026): raised the imager with a ladder rig and added baffles to the outer glass panes and the interior pane receiving direct sunlight. Clear, sunny, smoky sky. Usable images and spectra.

What the spectra show

  • C. glomerata: a chlorophyll-a signature. High green, then a dip and a dramatic rise in the red bands (the “red edge”).
  • Diatoms: a chlorophyll-a signature but no red dip, suggesting diatoms covering the algae and dimming the response.
  • Bare substrate: the color of the rock, with no defined dip.

The full story

The lab’s multispectral imager couldn’t be validated because there were no algal blooms in the field. I designed and built a 12″ × 24″ × 20″ recirculating mesocosm that recreates river conditions in the lab. It uses pump-driven flow over rocks to mimic current, and a dosing schedule I developed, starting from deionized water, to reproduce the river and bloom chemistry. I collected Cladophora glomerata from the Gallatin and Madison Rivers, confirmed the species with an improvised polarized-light microscope, and grew it in the tank.

To provide ground truth for the imager, I built a continuous water-quality monitoring system using an Arduino UNO R4 WiFi with conductivity, pH, turbidity, and temperature sensors. It ran continuously for two weeks, serving its own web dashboard with live readings and reports. I used chemistry models (Kohlrausch’s law, ionic strength, carbonate equilibria) to predict expected readings and compared them against measured values.

Finally, I took the tank into the field with a Pika L hyperspectral imager. The first deployment’s images were unusable because of sky glint, so I redesigned the setup with a raised ladder rig and baffled glass. The resulting spectra for algae (including the chlorophyll red edge), diatoms, and bare substrate matched prior field data captured with the same imager and calibration.
